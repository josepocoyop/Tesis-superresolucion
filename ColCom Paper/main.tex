\begin{abstract}
Conventional video surveillance cameras often capture sequences with low spatial resolution and reduced frame rates, which hinders scene analysis and forensic tasks. This paper evaluates a deep learning pipeline for the spatial and temporal enhancement of surveillance video that combines two existing pretrained models: ESRGAN for spatial super-resolution and RIFE for temporal frame interpolation. The contribution is the integration of both stages and its evaluation on CCTV footage under a reproducible degradation protocol, rather than a new architecture. Footage recorded with a commercial fixed surveillance camera at $848\times480$ pixels and 15~fps was degraded to $212\times120$ pixels following the standard Real-ESRGAN synthetic protocol. The pipeline is evaluated using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, a learned perceptual image quality metric, benchmarked against bicubic upscaling. ESRGAN reaches an LPIPS of 0.311 against 0.714 for bicubic upscaling, while its PSNR is lower, 18.84~dB against 20.42~dB, a behavior consistent with the perception-distortion tradeoff. RIFE doubles the output frame rate from 15 to 30~fps, and its synthesized frames reach 37.12~dB in a hold-out test against 33.95~dB for frame averaging. The complete pipeline runs at 52.5~ms per input frame and 360~MB of GPU memory on a desktop card, which keeps it applicable to existing surveillance installations without replacing hardware.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Video surveillance systems are a standard component of security in residential, commercial, and industrial settings. A large share of the installed base, however, runs on low-cost equipment or on specifications originally designed for limited bandwidth and storage, which produces video with low spatial resolution, digital noise, and loss of detail under poor lighting~\cite{li2011people}. These limitations reduce the ability to identify people, vehicles, and objects, and they cut the usefulness of CCTV both for live monitoring and for forensic analysis after an event.

Automatic video enhancement based on deep learning is a practical way around these constraints without replacing the installed infrastructure. In the spatial domain, deep convolutional networks learn to reconstruct textures and high-frequency edges from degraded images~\cite{dong2014srcnn, vanouwerkerk2006survey}. Generative adversarial networks (GANs)~\cite{goodfellow2014gan}, and SRGAN~\cite{ledig2017srgan} and ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} in particular, synthesize finer detail than conventional interpolation. In the temporal domain, frame interpolation based on deep optical flow estimation~\cite{dosovitskiy2015flownet}, such as Super~SloMo~\cite{jiang2018superslomo}, DAIN~\cite{bao2019dain}, and RIFE~\cite{huang2022rife}, generates coherent intermediate frames that raise the frame rate and improve visual continuity.

This paper evaluates a sequential pipeline that combines ESRGAN for frame-by-frame spatial restoration and RIFE for temporal interpolation between consecutive frames, applied to surveillance sequences degraded to $212\times120$~pixels with the Real-ESRGAN synthetic protocol.

The scope of the contribution is a narrow one. Both models already exist and are used with their pretrained weights, without changing their architectures or retraining them. What this work adds is the integration of the two stages into a single pipeline, its evaluation on footage from a commercial surveillance camera under a reproducible degradation protocol, and the measurement of the computational cost that this combination imposes on a desktop GPU. No new super-resolution or interpolation algorithm is proposed.

\section{Related Work}

Quality enhancement of surveillance video involves two complementary dimensions: spatial super-resolution and temporal interpolation. Both have been studied separately; their integration in the context of CCTV systems has received less attention in the literature.

\subsection{Spatial super-resolution}

Classical super-resolution methods relied on mathematical interpolation, such as bicubic and bilinear~\cite{vanouwerkerk2006survey}. Although computationally cheap, these approaches average adjacent pixels without modeling the statistical distribution of natural textures, and produce smoothed results that cannot recover edges and fine detail.

Dong et al.~\cite{dong2014srcnn} showed that a convolutional neural network (SRCNN) could learn the mapping between low- and high-resolution images, outperforming interpolation on objective metrics. Residual architectures~\cite{he2016resnet} later made deeper networks possible: EDSR~\cite{lim2017edsr} removed batch normalization from the generator, while RCAN~\cite{zhang2018rcan} added channel attention to weight the features of greatest spatial relevance.

The introduction of GANs~\cite{goodfellow2014gan} changed the approach by adding a discriminator that supervises the perceptual quality of the generated images. SRGAN~\cite{ledig2017srgan} was the first model to apply this scheme to $\times$4 super-resolution, together with a perceptual loss based on VGG activations~\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, originally proposed by Johnson et al.~\cite{johnson2016perceptual}. ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} extended that architecture with RRDB blocks, a relativistic discriminator, and an improved perceptual loss, and obtained more detailed textures. Real-ESRGAN~\cite{wang2021realesrgan} adapted the model to real-world degradations through complex synthetic training data, which makes it well suited to the low-quality sequences typical of CCTV systems.

\subsection{Video super-resolution}

Video super-resolution (VSR) exploits the temporal redundancy between consecutive frames to obtain better reconstructions than single-image super-resolution. EDVR~\cite{wang2019edvr} introduced alignment and fusion networks with deformable convolutions, aligning neighboring frames in feature space before fusion. BasicVSR~\cite{chan2021basicvsr} identified bidirectional feature propagation along the sequence as an essential component, with a better balance between quality and computational cost. These models, however, require several frames to be processed together, which adds latency and a memory demand that can be prohibitive in surveillance systems with limited resources.

\subsection{Temporal frame interpolation}

Optical flow estimation with deep networks, introduced by FlowNet~\cite{dosovitskiy2015flownet}, made it possible to compute dense displacements between frames in a differentiable way. Super~SloMo~\cite{jiang2018superslomo} generalized this approach to the synthesis of several intermediate frames through bidirectional optical flows. DAIN~\cite{bao2019dain} added scene depth estimates to improve interpolation in occluded regions, and obtained more coherent results on sequences with complex motion. RIFE~\cite{huang2022rife} simplified the architecture with IFNet, which estimates the intermediate flow directly without bidirectional decomposition, and applies knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling} for efficient training, reaching real-time interpolation without losing quality.

\subsection{Positioning of this work}

Despite the individual progress in spatial super-resolution and temporal interpolation, most methods address a single dimension of the problem. Low-cost CCTV systems suffer from poor resolution and a low frame rate at the same time, so improving only one of the two leaves a substantial part of the problem unsolved. This work integrates ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} and RIFE~\cite{huang2022rife} into a sequential pipeline aimed at the practical constraints of these systems, and characterizes it in terms of both image quality and computational cost.

\section{Methodology}

The pipeline processes each degraded video sequence through four stages applied at the level of individual frames. Combining spatial enhancement with temporal synthesis recovers both the resolution and the smoothness of the input video.

\subsection{Dataset Acquisition and Preparation}
\label{sec:dataset}

The evaluation was carried out on footage recorded by the authors with a commercial EZVIZ surveillance camera, mounted in a fixed position over an urban street, in daylight, at 15~fps and a native resolution of $848\times480$~pixels. This recording serves as the high-resolution (HR) reference.

To simulate the degradations of lower-quality CCTV systems, the Real-ESRGAN synthetic degradation protocol~\cite{wang2021realesrgan} was applied: Gaussian blur ($\sigma=1.5$), bicubic downsampling by a factor of $4\times$, JPEG compression (quality~50), and additive Gaussian noise ($\sigma=5$~DN). The protocol reduces the HR frames from $848\times480$ to low-resolution (LR) images of $212\times120$~pixels, and produces artifacts representative of low-end cameras. Sixty frames were used for the quantitative evaluation.

\subsection{Pipeline Architecture}

Each sequence goes through four sequential stages, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Frame extraction:} The input video is split into individual frames. For the quantitative evaluation, 60~frames were taken at uniform intervals along the recording, so that the measurement is not concentrated on a single moment of the scene. The temporal evaluation, in contrast, needs adjacent frames and was carried out on consecutive stretches of the video.

    \item \textbf{Spatial super-resolution:} Each LR frame is processed on its own by ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan}, whose architecture is detailed in Fig.~\ref{fig:esrgan}. The network reconstructs textures and raises the resolution by a factor of $4\times$, producing frames of $848\times480$~pixels.

    \item \textbf{Temporal interpolation:} The sequence of enhanced frames is passed to RIFE~\cite{huang2022rife}, described in Fig.~\ref{fig:rife}, which estimates the optical flow between consecutive frames and synthesizes one intermediate frame per pair, raising the frame rate by a factor of $2\times$.

    \item \textbf{Video reconstruction:} The enhanced and synthesized frames are assembled in chronological order to produce the output video with higher resolution and smoother motion.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implementation Environment}

The pipeline was implemented in Python~3.11 on an NVIDIA RTX~4070~Ti GPU (12~GB VRAM, CUDA~12.4). The main libraries are \textit{realesrgan}~0.3 for ESRGAN inference and the official ECCV2022-RIFE repository~\cite{huang2022rife} for temporal interpolation.

\subsection{Models Used}

\subsubsection{ESRGAN}

ESRGAN (\textit{Enhanced Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Network})~\cite{wang2018esrgan} is a generative adversarial network~\cite{goodfellow2014gan} trained with perceptual losses to reconstruct high-frequency detail from degraded images. Unlike its predecessor SRGAN~\cite{ledig2017srgan}, its generator uses residual-in-residual dense blocks (RRDB) derived from residual networks~\cite{he2016resnet}, and removes the batch normalization layers to avoid the artifacts they introduce in texture synthesis. The discriminator does not work as in a plain GAN either: instead of deciding whether an image is real or generated, the relativistic average discriminator (RaD) estimates how much more realistic the real image looks than the generated one, which pushes the generator towards more convincing textures. The loss combines a pixel-level term (L1), an adversarial term, and a perceptual term derived from VGG activations~\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, originally proposed by Johnson et al.~\cite{johnson2016perceptual}. Fig.~\ref{fig:esrgan} summarizes the generator and the three losses. This work uses the pretrained Real-ESRGAN weights without retraining, so only the generator runs at inference time.

The generator chains an initial convolution, 23 RRDB blocks of 64 channels with a global residual connection, upsampling in two $\times2$ steps, and the output convolutions. The three losses are used during training only; at inference the generator runs on its own.}

\subsubsection{RIFE}

RIFE (\textit{Real-time Intermediate Flow Estimation})~\cite{huang2022rife} is a frame interpolation model that estimates the intermediate optical flow between two consecutive frames directly, without the bidirectional decomposition used by earlier methods such as DAIN~\cite{bao2019dain} and Super~SloMo~\cite{jiang2018superslomo}. Its core component, IFNet, computes this flow at three successive scales, from coarse to fine. A knowledge distillation scheme~\cite{hinton2015distilling} guides the training of IFNet and lets it learn accurate motion representations efficiently. Along with the flow, IFNet produces a fusion mask. The intermediate frame is obtained by warping the two adjacent frames according to the flow and blending both versions with the weight given by the mask, which is the mechanism the model uses to resolve occluded areas, as sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:rife}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \input{figures/fig3_rife}
    \caption{RIFE architecture~\cite{huang2022rife}. IFNet estimates, at three successive scales, the intermediate flow $F$ and a fusion mask $M$. The flow warps the two input frames towards instant $t\!+\!0.5$ and the mask weights the blend of both versions, which is what resolves the occluded regions. Dashed lines mark the input frames that feed the warping stage.}
    \label{fig:rife}
\end{figure}

\section{Experimental Results}

The proposed pipeline was evaluated on the recording described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset} along three lines: the visual reconstruction, the quality of the frames added by the temporal interpolation, and the objective metrics against bicubic upscaling.

\subsection{Qualitative Evaluation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison} shows four versions of the same frame: the LR input ($212\times120$~px, scaled with nearest-neighbor), $\times$4 bicubic upscaling, the $\times$4 ESRGAN output, and the HR reference ($848\times480$~px). The bottom row magnifies a region of the facade, where the difference between methods is visible: bicubic reconstruction averages neighboring pixels and returns a blurred surface, while ESRGAN produces defined edges.

That magnified region deserves a careful reading. It contains a window grille, and the texture generated by ESRGAN is sharp but does not match the reference: the network reconstructs continuous vertical bars where the original has a grid of rectangles. In other words, the model restores high-frequency detail with a plausible appearance, not necessarily the detail that was present in the scene. This behavior explains the lower PSNR in Table~\ref{tab:metrics} and is the same one discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. For forensic use the distinction matters: the output helps guide observation, but it is not evidence of the exact shape of an object.

\subsection{Temporal Smoothness Evaluation}
\label{sec:temporal}

Adding RIFE doubles the frame rate from 15 to 30~fps. Fig.~\ref{fig:framerate} shows a pair of adjacent frames of the sequence, 66.7~ms apart, next to the frame that RIFE synthesizes between them.

The synthesized frames have no reference to be compared against, since they are inserted at instants the camera never recorded. To measure their quality, a hold-out test was used: from consecutive triplets $(t\!-\!1, t, t\!+\!1)$ of the original recording the middle frame was hidden, interpolated from the two outer frames, and the result compared with the real frame. The test was restricted to triplets with noticeable motion, defined as a mean absolute difference above 3~DN between the two outer frames, because on static stretches any method reproduces the hidden frame and the comparison says nothing. Over 23~triplets selected this way, RIFE reached 37.12~dB PSNR, 0.9836 SSIM, and 0.0111 LPIPS, against 33.95~dB, 0.9759, and 0.0161 for the average of the two neighboring frames. The test is also harder than the actual use: the two outer frames of the triplet are two capture intervals apart, whereas in the proposed pipeline the interpolation takes place between frames one interval apart.

\subsection{Quantitative Evaluation}

The objective quality of the spatial enhancement was measured with PSNR (\textit{Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio}) and SSIM (\textit{Structural Similarity Index}). PSNR relates the maximum signal power to the reconstruction error~\cite{huynhthu2008psnr}:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{MAX_I^{2}}{MSE}
    \label{eq:psnr}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $MAX_I$ is the maximum possible pixel value and $MSE$ the mean squared error between the reference and the reconstructed image.

SSIM evaluates the structural similarity between images, taking luminance, contrast, and structure together~\cite{wang2004ssim}:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SSIM}(x,y) =
        \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + c_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + c_2)}
             {(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + c_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + c_2)}
    \label{eq:ssim}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu$ is the mean, $\sigma_{xy}$ the covariance, and $c_1$, $c_2$ stabilizing constants.

Table~\ref{tab:metrics} summarizes the average results over the 60~evaluation frames, together with the computational cost of each method. The output frame rate column is the rate of the resulting video, which is different from the processing speed given in the last column. The three spatial metrics are always computed on the frames present in the original sequence, the only ones with an HR reference; that is why ESRGAN and the complete pipeline share those values and differ in output rate and processing time. The quality of the frames added by RIFE is reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:temporal}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that ESRGAN and RIFE address two distinct and complementary shortcomings of low-cost CCTV systems. While mathematical interpolation methods such as bicubic~\cite{vanouwerkerk2006survey} compute pixels by local averaging without recovering high-frequency spectral information, the RRDB generator of ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} synthesizes structural detail from latent representations, producing sharp edges and perceptually coherent textures. This advantage matters in surveillance sequences where recognizing faces, license plates, and clothing can be decisive in a forensic investigation.

As Table~\ref{tab:metrics} shows, ESRGAN obtains a PSNR of 18.84~dB against 20.42~dB for bicubic upscaling, a result consistent with the perception-distortion tradeoff documented by Blau and Michaeli~\cite{blau2018perception}: generative models trade pixel-level fidelity for perceptual quality. This reading is supported by LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, which evaluates perceptual similarity through deep network activations: ESRGAN reaches an LPIPS of 0.311 against 0.714 for bicubic upscaling, a reduction of 56~\% that reflects the model's ability to synthesize visually coherent structures that the local averaging of bicubic cannot recover. SSIM shows only a marginal difference (0.5194 against 0.5132), which indicates that the gain of the generator is concentrated in high-frequency detail whose contribution to the overall structural distortion is limited.

In the temporal domain, IFNet estimates the intermediate optical flow directly, a strategy that in earlier interpolation studies~\cite{jiang2018superslomo, bao2019dain} has been shown to produce smoother and more coherent transitions than plain frame averaging. The resulting higher frame rate improves the continuous representation of motion and makes object tracking easier for operators and for video analysis algorithms.

As for computational cost, ESRGAN takes 45.5~ms per frame and RIFE 14.0~ms per synthesized frame, measured on the RTX~4070~Ti with the model already loaded in memory. Since RIFE generates one frame per input pair, the complete pipeline takes 52.5~ms per input frame, that is, it processes 19.0~input frames per second. Peak video memory is 360~MB for ESRGAN and 274~MB for RIFE; because the stages run one after the other, the peak of the complete pipeline is the larger of the two and not their sum. The system therefore keeps up with a 15~fps camera in real time on a desktop GPU, with no need for cloud infrastructure, although the remaining margin is narrow for serving several cameras in parallel on the same machine.

These results should be read against the scope of the tests. The quantitative evaluation rests on 60~frames of a single outdoor daylight scene, enough to show the trend between methods but too small to support conclusions about how the system behaves under different conditions. Night-time and indoor material is missing in particular, where sensor noise and artificial lighting change the degradations considerably. The degradation was also generated with a single synthetic protocol of fixed parameters, whereas real cameras combine motion blur, compression artifacts that vary with the codec, and transmission losses; testing with more diverse degradations is needed to estimate the robustness of the system. On top of that, ESRGAN processes each frame independently, without using information from neighboring frames, which can produce texture variations between adjacent frames in the areas where the network synthesizes detail. Finally, RIFE is sensitive to abrupt lighting changes and to very fast motion, where halos or unnatural transitions can appear at object edges.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}

\subsection{Conclusions}

This paper evaluated a pipeline for improving the resolution and smoothness of CCTV video, based on the sequential integration of ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} ($4\times$ spatial super-resolution) and RIFE~\cite{huang2022rife} ($2\times$ temporal interpolation), on sequences degraded to $212\times120$~pixels with the Real-ESRGAN synthetic protocol~\cite{wang2021realesrgan}. The contribution is not a new model, but the combination of two pretrained models into a single pipeline and its characterization on footage from a commercial surveillance camera, in terms of both image quality and computational cost.

The quantitative evaluation over 60~frames shows that ESRGAN obtains an LPIPS of 0.311 against 0.714 for bicubic upscaling, a reduction of 56~\%, with a marginal SSIM difference (0.5194 against 0.5132) and a lower PSNR, 18.84~dB against 20.42~dB. This behavior is consistent with the perception-distortion tradeoff~\cite{blau2018perception}: the generator trades pixel-level fidelity for visually coherent textures that bicubic does not recover. In the temporal domain, the hold-out test over 23~triplets with motion places the frames synthesized by RIFE at 37.12~dB against 33.95~dB for the average of neighboring frames. The complete pipeline runs at 52.5~ms per input frame, with a peak of 360~MB of video memory on a desktop GPU, which keeps up with the capture rate of the camera without external infrastructure. The results were obtained on a single daylight scene, so they amount to an initial characterization rather than a validation under varied operating conditions.

\subsection{Future Work}

The immediate priority is to extend the evaluation set to night-time and indoor scenes, and to degradations more varied than the synthetic protocol used here, so that the reported figures can be contrasted under different operating conditions. Second, video super-resolution techniques such as BasicVSR~\cite{chan2021basicvsr} will be explored, since they exploit the coherence between neighboring frames and could reduce the texture variations that frame-by-frame processing introduces. Finally, an event detection module is planned so that the enhancement is triggered only on the segments of interest, which would cut the computational load and make it feasible to serve several cameras with one machine, along with optimizing the pipeline for embedded hardware.
